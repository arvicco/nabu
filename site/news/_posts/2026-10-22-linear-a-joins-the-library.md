The library now holds **Linear A** — the undeciphered administrative
script of Minoan Crete, c. 1800–1450 BCE. The SigLA palaeographic
database (Salgarella & Castellan, CC BY-NC-SA) arrives as 787
inscriptions and 3,097 sign groups from Haghia Triada, Khania,
Phaistos, Zakros and Knossos, every sign attestation classified —
syllabogram, logogram, fraction, erasure — and read by phonetic value
where one exists. The data ships as OCaml Marshal blobs inside a
JavaScript file; the new decoder reads the format natively and proves
itself by re-encoding both payloads byte-for-byte. A researched table
of Minoan ceramic phases (with the Thera high-chronology dispute
declared, not hidden) dates 760 of the 787 inscriptions; every one is
placed at its find-site.

Beside it, the first real **Old Uyghur dictionary**: Wilkens's
*Handwörterbuch des Altuigurischen* (Göttingen 2021, CC BY-SA), 49,619
entries glossed in German and Turkish, segmented from the printed
book's PDF text layer with entry stacks, column-crossing hyphen wraps
and homonym numbering preserved — and the arithmetic published: 49,619
accepted plus 2 named skips reconcile exactly against the 49,621
records the segmentation experiment measured last window.

Underneath, the quieter rails. The Hebrew Bible's clause and phrase
grammar now rides every span, the Dead Sea Scrolls' lexeme crosswalk
to it is censused at 99.98%, and the Middle German corpora recovered
annotation lanes a parser had been dropping. The parallels engine
gained a measuring stick — recall benchmarks over six ground-truth
sets, from inscription editions to Buddhist canon parallels, with
seeded baselines pinned so every future scoring tweak must prove
itself in numbers. And a convention now exists for any future
model-produced enrichment: content-addressed caching, full prompt
provenance, append-only cost ledgers and a monthly budget cap that
fails closed.
