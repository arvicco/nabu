Both of this month's correspondence grants are now shelves. The
library stands at **988,944 documents — 75.2 million passages — in
139 languages.**

**The Nordic manuscripts.** The Menota archive enters at manuscript
grain: 91 medieval codices and fragments, each word transcribed at
up to three levels — what the scribe drew, what the scribe wrote,
what the philologist reads. Nabu stores the diplomatic reading,
line by manuscript line: Laxdæla saga, the Codex Wormianus (whose
runic entities the file declares itself — the parser learned to
listen), Konungs skuggsjá, the Old Norwegian homily book, the
Landslǫg law manuscripts. Eight manuscripts that stumbled on their
first pass were recovered by reading the files more honestly, not
by guessing: every character mapping comes from upstream's own
bytes. And the language registry grew its Norwegian and Danish
ladders to receive them — sixty-two manuscripts now sit on their
language's own timeline, staged by nothing more than their
catalogue dates meeting the registry's period bands.

**The Middle English corpus.** From Michigan, the corpus behind the
Middle English Dictionary: 297 texts — the Canterbury Tales, Sir
Gawain and the Green Knight, the York and Towneley mystery cycles,
Piers Plowman in its versions, and the wills, gild records and
medical treatises that carry the working language. It rides a new
parser family built for the Text Creation Partnership's schema —
machinery deliberately shaped so that the sixty-thousand-text
EEBO-TCP world, whose door the same grant opened, can walk through
it one day.

Thanks are owed twice: to the Menota network, whose archive is
openly licensed and who blessed our polite crawl while their bulk
endpoint is built, and to the Middle English Compendium's editors,
who pointed us at files better than the ones we asked about.
