Two wings opened and a third began. The Middle Iranian shelf is now
complete as TITUS hosts it: five grant-gated corpora — the Pahlavi
books, the Manichaean corpora, Sogdian, Khotanese Saka and Bactrian —
joined Tocharian A in one wave, each retrieved once under its
personal grant, census-fixed against the real pages, and verified to
a clean parse. Not one of the 191 first-sync surprises (unknown
editorial lanes, severed UTF-8, the editors' own "not yet read"
entries) aborted a sync: every one quarantined honestly, got its
census, and recovered. Bactrian's dated documents now carry real CE
envelopes — era year by era year through the ruled [+222, +228]
window that spans the contested epoch, down to the Tang-i Safedak
inscription's year 492 — and 417 of the Tocharian A leaves know
their findspot.

The Slavic opening ran end to end across two phases. Nine wikisource
cones — Serbian epic song to Old East Slavic chronicle — censused
category by category with written exclusion rules (the Russian
cone's honest yield: 12 texts, after 31 pages keyed to in-copyright
editions were turned away). Then the completion wave: the KorBa
manually annotated Middle Polish subcorpus and the 1488 Prague Bible
ground truth; KDSP's long prose and the Protestant prints of Trubar
and Bohorič in page OCR; the Serbian ELTeC novels, the Croatian
school canon and the ijekavian Vuk–Daničić Bible, each work admitted
on its own public-domain record; the complete Ipatiev chronicle from
the 1908 Petersburg edition with the editors' Soviet-era volumes
excluded by rule; Nasovič's 1870 dictionary opening the Belarusian
shelf; and the German Slavistics canon — Miklosich to Jagić's
Archiv — as a reference shelf with its OCR quality measured per
volume. Riding along: Brückner's 1927 etymological dictionary of
Polish as a real, queryable shelf, and the registry grew its West
and South Slavic families with the Polish and Czech stage ladders.

And the Americas. The library's first American wing opens with the
Ticha corpus of colonial Valley Zapotec — Córdova's 1578 Arte
through two centuries of notarial testaments, each document postured
honestly as original or period translation — Guaman Poma's Nueva
corónica entire from the Royal Danish Library's public-domain
edition, and the Jesuit-era Tupinambá and Old Guaraní treebanks. The
registry minted the Oto-Manguean and Tupian families to carry them,
and the asks are out for the biggest prize of all: the full
machine-readable Florentine Codex.

By the numbers: twenty-four new text sources across the three
phases, two new dictionary shelves (Brückner's Polish etymologicum,
Nasovič's Belarusian), one new research desk, and the kotenseki
crawl past a quarter of its 97,731 books. The library stands, as of
this writing, at 2.35 million documents and 110 million passages
across 203 corpus sources on 28 desks (the [live
census]({{ '/library/' | relative_url }}) has since moved on) — and the whole wave
rode the quarantine belts
without a single aborted sync.

As always: canonical means canonical — every text verbatim from its
source, every exclusion a written rule, every license the grantor's
own words.
