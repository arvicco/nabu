  Two November days: 5,683 early medieval Latin charters from
  St. Gallen to Lucca, a three-source Mongolic shelf spanning Manchu,
  Middle Mongol and Classical Mongolian, and the first leg of the
  mass-digitization wave — the British Library's digitised books,
  DBNL's hand-corrected Dutch literature, and the opening cones of
  the Norwegian National Library and the Wellcome Collection.

Two November days of development bring the library to **2,873,127
documents / 130,175,120 passages across 336 live sources** (census
9 November 2026).

**The early Middle Ages, charter by charter.** The public-domain
charter editions of the Hamburg Formulae – Litterae – Chartae
project enter as 5,164 early medieval Latin charters and royal
diplomas, c. 700–1300 — St. Gallen and Freising the largest blocks,
beside Redon, Stavelot-Malmedy, the Marmoutier cartularies, the
Lombard charters and the diplomas of Pippin III and Charlemagne —
every charter carrying its editor's regest and dating, 1,984 their
issuing place, each file licensed by its own terms. Beside them the
Late Latin Charter Treebank brings 519 private charters of Lombard
and Carolingian Tuscany (AD 714–869, written mostly at Lucca), every
one of its ~226,000 words hand-annotated with lemma, morphology and
dependency syntax and marked formulaic or free. The wave also
hardened a dating law: a printed edition's year is now excluded from
language-stage inference everywhere, so a medieval charter printed
in 1879 — or a 1510 Paris printing of an ancient text — stages by
what it is, never by when it was typeset.

**The Mongolic shelf.** Three sources open Inner Asia's archival
languages: real Manchu text from multilingual Wikisource — the
Veritable Records, the Treaty of Nerchinsk, the Seven Grievances,
Qianlong's Ode to Mukden — in script and romanization with the
Literary Chinese layers beside them; Shiratori Kurakichi's romanized
interlinear Secret History of the Mongols (1943, 632 pages), a
second Middle Mongol witness beside the held Street edition; and the
public-domain reference canon for Manchu, Classical Mongolian and
Written Oirat — fifteen volumes from Zakharov's Manchu–Russian
dictionary to Kowalewski and Pozdneev.

**The mass-digitization wave, first leg.** Four national- and
research-library OCR collections came onto the shelves in one wave,
each at a declared cone with a resumable ledger, each honest about
what OCR is. The British Library's Digitised Books arrive at their
pre-1800 cone — 2,497 books / 230,206 passages, overwhelmingly
English drama and verse from the Elizabethan stage to the
eighteenth century's farces, raw period OCR declared as such, every
page carrying the OCR's own confidence score. DBNL, the digital
library of Dutch literature, lands as 3,653 public-domain titles /
950,870 passages of edited, hand-corrected transcription — from the
Hulthem manuscript's abele spelen through Hooft, Vondel and the
Golden Age to the nineteenth century, each title carrying DBNL's own
dating, genre and printing history. And two paced crawls opened
their cones: the National Library of Norway (everything pre-1700
plus all years of Old Norse, Latin, Greek and Sámi — the first slice
holds Verelius' *Index linguae veteris Scytho-Scandicae* of 1691)
and the Wellcome Collection (31,883 pre-1800 volumes censused,
13,262 of them Latin, filling Latin-first at the library's own
requested pace — the first slice holds 43 sixteenth-century Latin
medical volumes). The standing NDL Classical Books crawl passed
66,387 Japanese and Chinese books in the same window.

As always, the numbers above are the generated census of the live
catalog, not hand-kept claims.
