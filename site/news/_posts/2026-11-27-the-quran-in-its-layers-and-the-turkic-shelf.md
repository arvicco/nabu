**The Qurʾān as text and as grammar.** The Berlin-Brandenburg Academy's
Corpus Coranicum publishes the Cairo edition of 1924 with Rafael
Talmon's concordance: 114 suras, 6,236 verses, and for each of the
77,432 words its transliteration, root, part of speech and inflection,
with every alternative analysis kept. Beside it stands the Quranic
Arabic Corpus morphology (Kais Dukes, Leeds), which divides each word
into prefix, stem and suffix; its terms forbid altered copies, so it is
held for research only. Both answer grammatical filters — person,
gender, number, case, mood, voice, the verb forms I–XII.

**Early manuscripts, verse by verse.** The same project transcribes
early Qurʾān manuscripts word by word in their bare consonantal
script: 193 manuscripts, 32,182 verse passages, each lined up with the
Cairo text. Letters the editors supply, letters that are unclear and
letters a later hand changed are marked; 172 manuscripts carry a
copying date and 98 a recorded origin.

**The variant readings.** 18,000 readings ascribed to 752 readers of
the first Islamic centuries, one document per reader and one passage
per verse he reads differently, each word keyed to its place in the
verse; readers are dated by their death and placed by their city where
the project's authority file says so.

**From a word to Lane.** `nabu define --root` and `nabu show --roots`
step from a Qurʾānic word to the entries of Lane's Lexicon under its
root — typed in Arabic script or in transliteration. Nearly every
root-bearing word reaches a heading; the answer says when a match
needed the weak-letter allowances, and the handful of roots the
digitized Lane lacks are named as such.

**Reading Arabic and transliteration.** Vowel signs and recitation
marks are now separate display layers: the default keeps the vowels and
drops the pause signs, `plain` gives the bare consonants, `full` the
text as stored. Transliterated Arabic, Hebrew, Aramaic and Persian are
no longer set right to left.

**The Orkhon inscriptions.** The memorial stelae of Kül Tigin (732) and
Bilge Kağan (735) and the two stones of the statesman Tonyukuk — the
oldest substantial texts in any Turkic language — are held line by
line in the runiform script, from Turkish Wikisource: 214 lines, each
with a transcription and a modern Turkish translation, all three
searchable.

**Four founding books of Turkology.** Géza Kuun's 1880 edition of the
Codex Cumanicus — the Cuman glossaries, riddles and hymns that Italian
merchants and German friars wrote down around 1300, the library's first
Cuman text — with Thomsen's decipherment of the Orkhon script (1896),
Radloff's *Alttürkische Inschriften der Mongolei* (1895–99) and
Vámbéry's first edition of the Qutadgu Bilig (1870), as public-domain
scans read page by page. The text is uncorrected OCR: the Latin-script
prose and transcriptions can be searched, the Uyghur and runiform type
cannot.

**Turkic dictionaries and cognate sets.** Wiktionary's Ottoman Turkish
(10,016 entries) and Old Anatolian Turkish (607) join the Old Turkic,
Old Uyghur and Chagatai extracts already on the reference shelf. Two
published comparative datasets sit beside them: Savelyev and Robbeets's
cognate sets for 32 Turkic languages, and the Transeurasian sets of
Robbeets and colleagues across Turkic, Mongolic, Tungusic, Koreanic and
Japonic, with Old Turkic, Cuman, Middle Mongolian, Jurchen, Late Middle
Korean and Old Japanese among the witnesses.

**The Wolfenbüttel shelf, widened.** A per-edition review of the Herzog
August Bibliothek's digital editions admitted twelve more —
Grimmelshausen, Lessing's translations, Christian II of Anhalt's
diaries, a Middle Low German psalter, Hainhofer, Rist among them — and
the letters of early modern court musicians: 1,050 transcripts now,
from 472. Eighteen editions stay out, each for a stated reason — most
state no language or embed the editors' commentary in the text.

**Treebank stages and examples.** The Ashokan edicts, the Swedish New
Testament of 1526 and the Zhelekhivka-spelled Ukrainian prints now sit
on their own language stages. The [examples page]({{ '/examples/' | relative_url }})
gained walk-throughs for the Byzantinist, the Arabist, the epigrapher
of the German lands and the Turkologist.
