**SEAL — Sources of Early Akkadian Literature** (Michael P. Streck
and Nathan Wasserman, Jerusalem/Leipzig) is where Assyriology keeps
its poetry: the Old Babylonian Gilgamesh tablets, love lyrics,
incantations, hymns and laments of the third and early second
millennium BCE, each in an up-to-date scholarly transliteration
with the damage honestly bracketed. It is the edition of record for
this literature, and it now stands in the library — **408
compositions, 14,953 lines** — under the editors' written grant for
personal research use: local only, never redistributed, every
displayed line carrying the project's credit.

The ingestion respected the edition's variety the hard way. A first
crawl parsed only the corner of the corpus the samples had shown;
the full census then taught the parser four page shapes the site
actually serves — tabular scores with witness sigla, labeled
paragraphs, free prose — and the steady state reads every text page
the archive publishes, with three broken-markup pages quarantined
by name rather than served with holes.

On the dictionary side, the **Coptic lexicon gained its second
etymological witness**: the KELLIA project's Egyptian-etymologies
table now rides beside the ORAEC crosswalk — two independent
scholarly witnesses to each Coptic word's descent through Demotic
and hieroglyphic Egyptian, merged where they agree (they never
disagreed: a zero-conflict census preceded the merge) and credited
separately where each speaks alone.
