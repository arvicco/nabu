After a quiet week the library ran four windows in one evening, and
the shelves show it: **{{ site.data.census.documents_display }}
documents / {{ site.data.census.passages_display }} passages** across
{{ site.data.census.live_sources }} live sources (as of
{{ site.data.census.as_of }}).

**The Pali canon arrived whole.** The Sri Lankan tripitaka.lk edition
— 563 documents, 456,967 passages of Pali with its Sinhala
translation interleaved — joined the Theravada shelf, and the
registry now records that its Pali is written in Sinhala script, so
script-aware search and collation treat it honestly. Beside it the
Mahāvyutpatti, the ninth-century translator's dictionary, came in as
a four-way bridge: 23,742 crosswalk edges joining Sanskrit, Tibetan,
Chinese and Mongolian Buddhist vocabulary to the dictionaries already
on the shelves.

**The alignment hub grew by a Testament.** The Bohairic Coptic Old
Testament aligned against the hub's other witnesses — 20 of 24 books,
10,493 verses, 64,188 new verse pairs, with the held-out books
documented rather than forced — and the work uncovered a wiring-era
defect: the Bohairic New Testament's zero-padded chapter numbers had
kept it out of co-alignment entirely. Folding the citation forms
restored 32,894 pairs that should have been there all along, and the
alignment index now rebuilds itself whenever its registry changes.

**Old Frisian went live.** The Corpus Oudfries — 177 witnesses of
medieval Frisian law, 14,136 passages, 235,462 tokens with lemma and
part of speech on every word — was reconciled exactly against the
publisher's own index. The Seventeen Statutes open the collection
with the classic line *Thit is thiv forme liodkest*; manuscript dates
and attributed composition eras ride separate timeline claims, and
region, dialect and genre are all mined. It joins under the same
research-private posture as the project's other Dutch-institute
corpora: readable at the desk, never redistributed.

**Verify-first paid for itself twice.** A suspected abbreviation
misread in the South Indian inscription shelf turned out to be
something better: the editors bracket damaged final digits ("Ś.
107[3]"), and the parser had been stopping at the bracket — reading
Śaka 1073 as Śaka 107, a thousand-year error across exactly 48
documents, corroborated against the inscriptions' own 60-year cycle
names and now fixed with the lost digits read as honest decade
ranges. And a hypothesis that trailing numerals in Egyptian
dictionary spellings were undercounted repeat signs was *refuted* the
same way — cross-examining two held corpora by token id showed they
are numeral signs in their own right, so the sign tally stands.

**Two more person authorities.** The Digital Prosopography of the
Roman Republic brought 4,876 persons of the Republic into the person
index under its stated non-commercial license, joining the Chinese
biographical database on the authority shelf; the Neo-Assyrian
prosopography network (17,387 persons under CC BY-SA) is verified and
queued next.

**And the reading instruments kept widening.** The Early New High
German reference corpus opened its morphology to search: a census of
all 190 held annotation files (2,061,753 tags) showed a closed
vocabulary that now decodes into the same case/number/gender/tense
names every other tagset uses — syncretic genders honestly searchable
from both sides. Dead Sea Scrolls words now show the English gloss of
their biblical-Hebrew lexeme, borrowed at read time through the
editors' own lexeme crosswalk (consonantal homographs kept apart).
The Old Uyghur dictionary's Manichaean, Christian and script marks
became browsable facets; Chinese characters gained phonetic-series
neighbours on the character card from the reconstructed Old Chinese
shelf; and the Greek treebanks' dialect labels — Attic, Ionic, Doric,
Aeolic — now compose with their date bands, so a search can ask for
Ionic prose of the classical period and mean both halves.
