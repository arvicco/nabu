title: "The Greek canon, and four first books"
date: 2026-11-13 00:00:00 +0000
description: >-
  Ancient Greek roughly quadruples with the freed TLG canon, philosophy
  becomes a first-class category, and four languages arrive from their
  first printed books: Old Prussian, Old Lithuanian, Old Albanian and
  Old Frisian.
---

Two waves landed since the index work, and both change what you can
ask the library.

**Ancient Greek roughly quadrupled.** The TLG E canon — the reference
corpus of Greek literature that scholarship has cited for decades, now
freed as open text — is searchable here: nearly 6,300 works from Homer
to the Byzantine chroniclers, about 5,000 of them held nowhere else in
the library. Epic, lyric, drama, historiography, medicine, mathematics,
the Fathers, the scholia and the fragmentary authors, carrying the
canon's own century datings and author classifications onto the
timeline. Greek is now the library's fifth-largest language, and the
Sources Chrétiennes patristic editions stand beside the canon. One
consequence got its own ruling: **philosophy is now a first-class
category** — Plato's tradition, the commentators and the doxographers,
nearly nine hundred documents, browsable as their own kind instead of
sitting unclassified.

**Four languages arrived from their first printed books**, in TITUS's
diplomatic editions (Frankfurt), each line as printed:

- **Old Prussian** — the complete written record of the extinct West
  Baltic language: both catechisms, the Enchiridion, the Elbing
  vocabulary, the fragments.
- **Old Lithuanian** — from Mažvydas's Katekizmas, the first printed
  Lithuanian book, through the Wolfenbüttel Postil's manuscript pages
  to Donelaitis's *Metai*.
- **Old Albanian** — from Buzuku's 1555 *Missale*, the first Albanian
  book, through Bardhi's dictionary to Bogdani, with their Latin,
  Italian, Turkish and Greek parallels riding as annotations.
- **Old Frisian** — the medieval law of the Frisian lands: the
  Hunsingo manuscripts, the Skeltana Riucht, the Brokmer Recht.

Every page of these editions parses cleanly — each print's side lanes
(a German hymn source, a marginal verse number, an editor's modern
rendering) was read, classified and kept as its own annotation rather
than dropped or mixed into the text. Search any of them and the
edition's credit line rides with the hit.
