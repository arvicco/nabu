  One development window: the complete English Suda, the Hill Museum's
  Syriac and Ethiopic manuscript catalogue, the Patristic Text Archive,
  the Sixth Council Pāḷi canon with its printed apparatus, 312 Ming

A single development window brings the library to **2,629,265
documents / 121,743,954 passages across 301 live sources** (census
3 November 2026) — thirty-two sources wired in one window, and the
dictionary layer past four million entries (4,203,984).

**Byzantium answers in English.** The complete English translation of
the Suda — the 10th-century Byzantine lexicon-encyclopedia, 31,341
entries keyed by Adler number with translators' notes and vetting
history — joins the shelves beside the held First1K Greek text, from
the Suda On Line's own export. Beside it, the [Patristic Text
Archive](https://pta.bbaw.de) brings 113 open critical editions of
early Christian Greek: Severian of Gabala and the Pseudo-Chrysostom
corpus, Athanasius and the documents of the Arian controversy,
Eusebius, Origen, Hippolytus, each edition carrying its composition
date and places onto the library's axes.

**Eighteen thousand Eastern Christian manuscripts, catalogued.** The
Hill Museum & Manuscript Library's Reading Room dataset lands as a
manuscript catalogue source: 18,892 Syriac and Ethiopic manuscripts,
prints and archival objects — shelf marks, dated parts, origins,
scripts, contents — with 11,196 incipits in Syriac, Ge'ez, Amharic and
Arabic Garshuni as searchable passages, every record dated by the
catalogue's own envelope and placed by its origin, never its holding
repository.

**The Pāḷi canon with its apparatus.** The Sixth Buddhist Council
Tipiṭaka in the Yangon romanised edition arrives whole: 118 volumes —
canon, commentary and subcommentary — 89,512 paragraphs, with the
printed footnote apparatus parsed per paragraph: 73,431 variant
readings with their witness sigla. The Ṭīkā layer and the apparatus
are new to the library. With it comes the [Digital Pāḷi
Dictionary](https://digitalpalidictionary.github.io) (89,763 entries),
so the canon and its lookup layer land in the same window.

**The Ming examination hall.** 312 printed records of the Ming
civil-service examinations, 1371–1684, from the Tianyige collection in
Ningbo — examiners, the questions of the three sessions, graduate
rosters, model answers with the examiners' comments — 38,711 pages of
machine-read text (uncorrected OCR, declared as such), every record
dated by its reign era and the provincial records placed by province.

**The Pacific opens.** The library's first Oceanic and Micronesian
running text: the 1868 Hawaiian Bible in its period spelling, the
American Bible Society's Chamorro Gospels and the 1922 Pohnpeian New
Testament — 100 books / 48,762 verses. Beside them, thirteen Pacific
mission prints enter the local library as scanned editions: Grey's
*Ko Nga Moteatea* of 1853, Māori, Samoan, Tongan, Rarotongan, Fijian
and Tahitian Bibles of 1838–1868, and Williams' Māori dictionary —
the Google-digitized prints with their page text, the two image-only
scans catalogued as reference artifacts.

**The dictionary wave.** Twenty-six dictionary shelves in one window:
Söderwall's medieval Swedish dictionary with its supplement and
Schlyter's glossary to the old Swedish laws (51,811 entries, keyed to
the held fornsvenska corpus), Littré's *Dictionnaire de la langue
française* (78,483 articles whose HISTORIQUE sections quote Old and
Middle French century by century), the Mahan Kosh — Bhai Kahan Singh
Nabha's 1930 encyclopedic dictionary of the Gurbani vocabulary, 64,218
Gurmukhi entries for the held Sikh scripture — and twenty-two
Wiktionary extracts closing long-standing
lookup gaps: Middle English for the held Corpus of Middle English,
Old Norse, Gothic, Old and Middle French, Scots, Syriac, Aramaic,
Breton, Middle Dutch, Middle High German, Old Spanish, Old Swedish,
Old Occitan and more, each with its etymon and descendant trees.

As always, the numbers above are the generated census of the live
catalog, not hand-kept claims.
