**[Nabu v1.5.0](https://github.com/arvicco/nabu/releases/tag/v1.5.0)**
bundles the last four phases — the Korean desk, the instrument
honesty work, the Latin and Romance wave, and the closing polish —
into one release: **988,556 documents, 73.6 million passages, 134
language codes.**

The headline of the polish phase is **time**. The library now reads
dating in whatever form each source ships it — a German manuscript
catalog's "1487/88" and its century-half grid, the Patrologia's
author life bands, the Joseon annals' per-day entry dates, even
exact Seleucid-era arithmetic for Babylonian scholarship — and turns
all of it into honest machine bounds: **821,916 documents dated**,
with a new [chronological browse](/dates/) spanning 55 centuries.
And because dates compose with the language registry's period bands,
the closing pass placed 128,051 documents onto their language's own
timeline — sixty-three thousand classical Latin inscriptions among
them — with no human touching a single row.

Search grew honest edges to match: queries no longer leak one
script's transliteration rules into another's (a Portuguese search
stopped reaching Romanian through Old Church Slavonic), and scoped
searches now ride inside the index itself, so hunting a phrase in
one Korean chronicle among a million documents actually looks at
that chronicle.

Beside the library, the language registry it leans on —
**[nabu-lects](https://arvicco.github.io/nabu-lects)** — cut its own
first release:
**[v1.0.0](https://github.com/arvicco/nabu-lects/releases/tag/v1.0.0)**,
110 anchors and 68 historical stages under a semantic-versioning
contract on the identifier space itself. The release week settled a
question the small languages had been waiting on: **where do
dialects live?** Answer: as typed varieties with their
internationally registered names — the six dialects of Occitan and
Aranese, the Val d'Aran's own Gascon, are the first through the
door. Hittite finally got its anchor too, with thirty-six thousand
tablets behind it.

The library and its registry now version together — and the next
waves (the Menota Nordic manuscripts, the Corpus of Middle English)
already have their doors open.
